Read the model · assumptions, equations and references

A defined exchange rule

Choose two distinct agents uniformly. The first pays the second a uniformly random fraction of its current balance, up to its group’s cap. Agents cannot borrow. The exchange conserves money; goods and services are not represented.

payment = random(0, 1) × payer’s cap × payer’s balance

Group identity stays fixed. A low cap limits outflows without reducing the chance of receiving money. That gives low-cap groups a structural advantage in a mixed economy; it is part of this rule.

What the charts measure

These are cash-balance distributions, not measured incomes, net wealth or welfare. Gini 0 means equality; a value close to 1 means concentration. The charts rescale automatically; “Mean = 1” removes the monetary scale. The distribution chart keeps the same percentage-height scale when an illustrative payment is added.

The run history records bank balances. Summary cards, the group table and main chart include the chosen display transformation. A pinned comparison preserves its own transformation.

Three distinct payment experiments

Illustration: display each current bank balance plus the same amount. The added amount is outside the trading loop. It shifts the distribution horizontally and leaves absolute gaps unchanged, while reducing relative inequality: G′ = G × mean / (mean + payment).

Recurring: add the same amount to every bank balance before each round. This is newly created money, which can then be traded. There is no tax funding, production or inflation model. Changing this setting starts a new run.

Rescale: multiply displayed amounts by one factor. The monetary axis changes but Gini and money shares do not. This is a change of scale, not an income payment.

What the experiment can establish

It tests consequences of these assumptions. It cannot establish that budgeting dominates inequality in real economies, nor rule out oppression or policy effects. Different exchange rules can give different distributions. In particular, an exponential distribution has Gini 0.5; this payer-fraction rule gives about 0.63 at a 100% cap.

Research and provenance

  • Drăgulescu & Yakovenko (2000), Statistical mechanics of money. Includes exchange-rule dependence and departures from Gibbs behaviour.
  • Chakraborti & Chakrabarti (2000), How saving propensity affects its distribution. A distinct pooled-exchange model.
  • Yakovenko & Rosser (2009), Statistical mechanics of money, wealth, and income. Review of the models and their limitations.
  • Tao et al. (2019), Exponential structure of income inequality: evidence from 67 countries. Evidence concerns the low- and middle-income bulk, not a universal whole-wealth distribution.
